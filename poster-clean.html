AI-ccent Judgements:
Accent Stereotypes Persist with AI-Generated Voices

Jun Liu | University of Zurich | IAFPA 2026

Department of Computational Linguistics | jun.liu@uzh.ch

Listeners evaluate AI-generated voices not only through acoustic realism, but also through sociolinguistic meanings attached to accent.

Background

AI-generated voices are increasingly realistic but are not necessarily perceived as socially neutral. Listeners use accent to infer identity, credibility, competence, and social character. Accent stereotypes may also shape judgements of speaker traits and behaviour in forensic contexts (Paver et al., 2025), while synthetic speech creates challenges involving deception, impersonation, and audio-deepfake detection (Almutairi & Elgibreen, 2022).

Research Gap

Little is known about how accent, voice gender, and listener background jointly shape social evaluations of voices that listeners know are AI-generated.

Research Questions

  1. RQ1. Do standard and regional AI-generated accents differ in perceived trustworthiness, professionalism, and human-likeness?
  2. RQ2. Does perceived voice gender influence these evaluations?
  3. RQ3. Do British and American listeners evaluate British- and American-accented AI voices differently?

Method

Stimuli

  • 16 ElevenLabs-generated voices
  • 64 audio clips
  • Four accent varieties

Participants

  • 40 native English-speaking listeners
  • 20 British and 20 American

Rating Task

  • All participants rated all 64 clips
  • Four 6-point scales

Dataset

2,560 observations

Statistical Analysis

Beta mixed-effects regression

  • Ratings rescaled to (0,1)
  • Fixed effects: accent type, voice gender, listener nationality, accent nationality, and listener nationality × accent nationality
  • Crossed random intercepts: participant and audio file

Supplementary analyses:

  • Self-reported AI exposure
  • Perceived audio naturalness

Finding 1 — Accent Type

Regional accents were rated significantly higher than standard accents for trustworthiness and human-likeness, but not professionalism.

Finding 3 — Voice Gender

Voice gender did not significantly predict any outcome.

  • Trustworthiness: b = −0.019, p = .815
  • Professionalism: b = −0.106, p = .177
  • Human-likeness: b = 0.060, p = .622

Supplementary Analysis

Perceived audio naturalness positively predicted:

  • Trustworthiness: b = 1.037, p < .001
  • Professionalism: b = 0.776, p < .001
  • Human-likeness: b = 1.578, p < .001

Self-reported AI exposure was included as a robustness check. Its inclusion did not materially alter the main pattern of findings.

Overall Pattern

Accent was a stronger evaluative cue than voice gender, while perceived audio naturalness was strongly associated with all three outcomes.

Discussion

Synthetic voices were not evaluated as socially neutral. Accent continued to carry social meaning even when listeners knew the voices were artificially generated.

The effects were dimension-specific: no single voice profile simultaneously optimised trustworthiness, professionalism, and human-likeness.

Implications

  • Standard accents should not automatically be treated as neutral or superior defaults.
  • Voice selection should be tested with intended listener groups.
  • AI voices may reproduce existing sociolinguistic hierarchies.
  • Evaluation should distinguish social perception from perceived audio quality.

Forensic Relevance

Accent stereotypes can affect perceptions of speaker traits and behaviour in forensic contexts (Paver et al., 2025). Increasingly realistic synthetic voices also create challenges for detecting impersonation and audio deepfakes (Almutairi & Elgibreen, 2022).

This study did not directly test legal decision-making.

Limitations

  • Modest sample
  • One TTS platform
  • Possible voice-specific acoustic confounds
  • Single-item measures
  • Isolated, non-forensic listening context

Conclusion

AI-generated voices are evaluated through both acoustic and social perception. Accent remains a powerful cue in how listeners judge synthetic speech.

Accent stereotypes persist—even when the voice is artificial.
