AI-ccent Judgements: Accent Stereotypes Persist with AI-Generated Voices

Jun Liu · Department of Computational Linguistics, University of Zurich · jun.liu@uzh.ch
IAFPA Conference 2026 · 26–29 July · Trier, Germany
IAFPA
International Association for Forensic Phonetics and Acoustics
Takeaway: Listeners evaluate AI-generated voices not only through acoustic realism, but also through sociolinguistic meanings attached to accent.

Background and research gap

AI-generated voices are increasingly realistic but are not necessarily perceived as socially neutral. In human speech, listeners use accent to infer identity, credibility, competence, and social character. Accent stereotypes may also shape judgements of speaker traits and behaviour in forensic contexts (Paver et al., 2025), while increasingly realistic synthetic speech creates challenges involving deception, impersonation, fraud, and audio-deepfake detection (Almutairi & Elgibreen, 2022).

Research gap: Little is known about how accent, perceived voice gender, and listener background jointly shape social evaluations of voices that listeners know are AI-generated.

Scope caution: The experiment examined social evaluations of AI-generated voices. It did not directly test forensic evidence evaluation, legal decision-making, transcription accuracy, speaker identification, or deepfake-detection accuracy.

Research questions

  1. RQ1 — Accent type: How does accent type (standard vs. regional) influence ratings of trustworthiness, professionalism, and human-likeness in AI-generated voices?
  2. RQ2 — Voice gender: Does perceived voice gender influence ratings of trustworthiness and professionalism? Exploratorily, does it influence human-likeness?
  3. RQ3 — Listener and accent nationality: Does the relationship between accent nationality and listener nationality influence ratings of trustworthiness, professionalism, and human-likeness?

Participants

  • Final analysed sample: 40 participants — 20 British listeners and 20 American listeners.
  • All reported English as their first language and normal hearing.
  • Age range: 21–40 years; M = 31.80, SD = 5.43.
  • Gender: 21 female, 18 male, 1 preferred not to report.
  • All included participants passed the headphone screening and attention checks.
  • Study duration ≈ 20 minutes; compensation £3.

Stimuli, measures and procedure

voicesratingsprocedure

  • 16 unique ElevenLabs voices and 64 clips in total.
  • Within each of the four accent varieties, two male and two female voices.
  • Each voice produced four utterances; each participant rated all 64 clips.
  • Same four utterances across all conditions: two from Comma Gets a Cure, two from The North Wind and the Sun.
  • ElevenLabs settings: Stability 60%, Similarity Enhancement 75%, Speech Speed 1.0.
  • Audio: MP3, 44.1 kHz, 128 kbps; normalised to −23 LUFS.
  • Four separate 6-point ratings: trustworthiness, professionalism, human-likeness, perceived audio naturalness (no neutral midpoint; higher = more positive).
  • Procedure: consent & background questionnaire → headphone screening (6 trials, ≥5 correct, 1 retry allowed) → main task (64 clips, randomised order, up to 3 plays each) → 4 ratings per clip + attention checks every 6 clips → post-task quality checks + optional open-ended questions.

Analytical dataset: 40 participants × 64 clips = 2,560 observations, no missing values on analysis variables.

Statistical analysis

Ratings were rescaled from the original 1–6 scale to the open interval (0,1) and analysed using beta mixed-effects regression. Participant and audio file were included as crossed random intercepts; estimates are reported on the logit scale.

  • Main-model fixed effects: accent type, perceived voice gender, listener nationality, accent nationality, and listener nationality × accent nationality.
  • AI exposure robustness models: standardised self-reported AI exposure added as covariate.
  • Perceived-audio-naturalness models: standardised perceived audio naturalness added as predictor, retaining main fixed/random structure.

Interpretation rule: Do not compare regression estimates directly with raw 1–6 means. Regression estimates are on the logit scale; descriptive/estimated marginal means are on the original rating scale only where explicitly stated.

Causal caution: Perceived audio naturalness was measured, not manipulated. The coefficients show associations and should not be described as causal effects or mediation.

Sensitivity-model changes: After naturalness was included, the regional advantage for trustworthiness was no longer significant; the human-likeness regional advantage remained but was reduced; standard accents were rated significantly higher for professionalism; and female voices were rated significantly higher for human-likeness. These changes require cautious interpretation and are secondary to the preregistered/main model pattern.

Discussion and implications

  • Synthetic voices were not evaluated as socially neutral: listeners continued to respond to accent-based social meanings even when they knew that the voices were AI-generated.
  • The effects were dimension-specific. Regional accents showed advantages for trustworthiness and human-likeness, but accent type did not significantly predict professionalism in the main model.
  • Perceived voice gender was a comparatively weak cue in this disembodied AI-voice context.
  • Human-likeness emerged as a distinct evaluative construct: strongest accent-type effect, only significant listener-nationality × accent-nationality interaction in the main models, and strongest association with perceived audio naturalness.
  • There was no symmetrical own-accent preference. British-accented voices showed a broader advantage for trustworthiness and professionalism across both listener groups.

Practical implications

  • There is no single voice profile that simultaneously optimises trustworthiness, professionalism, and human-likeness.
  • Standard accents should not automatically be treated as neutral, safe, or superior defaults.
  • AI voice selection and localisation should be empirically tested with intended listener groups and evaluated across multiple social dimensions.
  • AI voice systems may reproduce existing sociolinguistic hierarchies if they privilege a narrow set of prestige-associated voices.
  • Perceived audio naturalness should be measured explicitly, while remaining conceptually distinct from human-likeness.

Forensic relevance — cautious framing

Accent stereotypes can shape perceptions of speaker traits and behaviour in forensic contexts (Paver et al., 2025). Increasingly realistic synthetic voices also create challenges for impersonation and audio-deepfake detection (Almutairi & Elgibreen, 2022). The present findings suggest that judgements of synthetic-voice credibility, authenticity, and humanness may reflect sociolinguistic expectations as well as perceived audio quality.

Do not overclaim: This experiment did not directly investigate courts, police transcripts, juror decisions, speaker identification, forensic transcription, or deepfake-detection accuracy.

Limitations

  • The between-participant sample was modest: 40 listeners, 20 in each nationality group.
  • Only British and American native-English listeners were included; generalisability to other national, linguistic, and L2 groups is limited.
  • Only four accent varieties were tested, all voices from one TTS platform.
  • Accent and perceived voice gender may have been partly confounded with voice-specific acoustic properties.
  • Each evaluative construct was measured with a single 6-point item.
  • Participants evaluated brief clips without an interactive, role-based, or forensic context.

Conclusion

AI-generated voices are not socially neutral. Regional accents were perceived as more trustworthy and human-like, while British-accented voices received broader advantages in trustworthiness and professionalism. AI-voice evaluation is shaped by sociolinguistic meaning, listener background, self-reported AI exposure, and perceived audio naturalness.

Accent stereotypes persist — even when the voice is artificial.
